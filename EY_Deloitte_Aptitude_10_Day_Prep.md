# Campus Aptitude Test: 10-Day Revision and Practice Guide

**Target:** Timed, 60 to 90 minute online aptitude assessments for engineering freshers, including tests used by firms such as EY and Deloitte.

**Scope:** 25 topics, 50 worked examples, 125 practice questions, consolidated answer key, test strategy, and a 10-day plan.

**Important:** Employer assessments vary by role, campus, test provider, question count, section timing, marking scheme, and permitted calculator use. This is a preparation guide, not a claim about a particular EY or Deloitte paper.

## How to use this file

1. Read each topic's concept and shortcuts. Work through the two solved examples without a calculator where possible.
2. Attempt its five questions before checking the **Answer key** at the end. Record mistakes by cause: concept, calculation, reading, or time.
3. Time the questions from Day 3 onward. Revisit a missed question after one day without viewing its solution.
4. For graphs, interpret the drawn bars using the stated scale. For verbal reasoning, answer only from the text provided.

**Timing guide:** A quantitative question may require 45 to 90 seconds, reasoning 30 to 75 seconds, and verbal 30 to 90 seconds depending on passage length. These are starting targets, not fixed limits.

# Part I. Quantitative Aptitude

## Q01. Percentages

**Core concept.** Percent means “out of 100.” Always identify the base before finding a percentage change. A percentage increase followed by an equal percentage decrease does not restore the original value.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- `x% of y = (x/100) × y`; often faster: `x% of y = y% of x`.
- `% change = (new - old) / old × 100`.
- Successive changes `a%` and `b%`: net percentage `= a + b + ab/100`, using negative signs for decreases.
- Equal increase and decrease of `r%`: net change `= -(r²/100)%`.
- Memorize: `12.5% = 1/8`, `16 2/3% = 1/6`, `20% = 1/5`, `25% = 1/4`, `33 1/3% = 1/3`.

**Solved example 1.** A price rises 20%, then falls 20%. Net change?  
**Fastest method:** Equal opposite changes give `-(20²/100) = -4%`. Final price is **96% of original**.

**Solved example 2.** A count rises from 640 to 800. Increase percentage?  
**Fastest method:** Increase `160`; `160/640 = 1/4`. Answer: **25%**.

**Common trap:** Dividing by the final value instead of the original when calculating an increase.

**Practice**

- **Q01.1** Find 15% of 240.
- **Q01.2** A price increases 25%, then decreases 20%. Find the net percentage change.
- **Q01.3** 72 is 30% of what number?
- **Q01.4** Sales increase from 400 to 460. Find the percentage increase.
- **Q01.5** A earns 20% more than B. B earns what percent less than A?

## Q02. Profit, Loss, Marked Price, Discount

**Core concept.** Profit and loss compare selling price (SP) with cost price (CP). Discounts compare SP with marked price (MP). These percentages use different bases, so keep them separate.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- `Profit = SP - CP`; `Loss = CP - SP`.
- `Profit% = profit/CP × 100`; `Loss% = loss/CP × 100`.
- `SP = CP(1 + p/100)` for profit; `SP = CP(1 - l/100)` for loss.
- `SP = MP(1 - d/100)` after discount `d%`.
- For sequential discounts, multiply remaining fractions instead of adding discount percentages.

**Solved example 1.** CP is ₹500. MP is 40% above CP and discount is 10%. Find profit percentage.  
**Fastest method:** `SP/CP = 1.40 × 0.90 = 1.26`, so profit = **26%**.

**Solved example 2.** An article sold for ₹640 at a 20% loss. Find CP.  
**Fastest method:** ₹640 represents 80% of CP, so `CP = 640/0.8 = ₹800`.

**Common trap:** Calculating discount on CP, or profit percentage on SP when the question does not say so.

**Practice**

- **Q02.1** CP ₹800, SP ₹920. Find profit percentage.
- **Q02.2** MP ₹1,200, discount 15%. Find SP.
- **Q02.3** CP ₹250, desired profit 20%. Find SP.
- **Q02.4** SP ₹900 at a 10% loss. Find CP.
- **Q02.5** Profit equals 25% of SP. Profit is what percentage of CP?

## Q03. Ratio and Proportion

**Core concept.** A ratio compares quantities in the same units. A proportion says two ratios are equal. For sharing totals, count ratio parts; for chained ratios, make the shared quantity equal.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- If `A:B = m:n` and `A + B = T`, then `A = Tm/(m+n)`, `B = Tn/(m+n)`.
- `a:b = c:d` means `ad = bc` when denominators are nonzero.
- To combine `A:B` and `B:C`, use the LCM of B's two ratio values.
- For fixed work with identical worker productivity, `workers × days` is constant.

**Solved example 1.** Share ₹640 in the ratio `3:5`.  
**Fastest method:** 8 parts, each ₹80. Shares: **₹240 and ₹400**.

**Solved example 2.** `A:B = 2:3` and `B:C = 4:5`. Find `A:B:C`.  
**Fastest method:** Make B equal to 12: first ratio `8:12`, second `12:15`. Answer: **8:12:15**.

**Common trap:** Adding denominators from unrelated ratios without first aligning the common term.

**Practice**

- **Q03.1** Split 550 in the ratio `4:7`.
- **Q03.2** Split 720 in the ratio `2:3:4`.
- **Q03.3** Twelve equally productive workers finish a job in 18 days. How many days will nine workers need?
- **Q03.4** If `A:B = 5:6` and `A = 45`, find B.
- **Q03.5** Milk and water are in the ratio `3:2` in a 25 L mixture. Add 5 L water. Find the new ratio.

## Q04. Averages

**Core concept.** An average is the total divided by the number of observations. Most fast average problems become simple after converting the average back into a total.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- `Average = sum/count`; therefore `sum = average × count`.
- Added item `x`: `new average = (old total + x)/(n+1)`.
- Replaced item `a` by `b`: average changes by `(b-a)/n`.
- Weighted mean of groups: `(n1×avg1 + n2×avg2)/(n1+n2)`.

**Solved example 1.** Six scores average 15. Remove a score of 21. Find the new average.  
**Fastest method:** Total `6×15 = 90`; remaining `69`. Average = `69/5 = 13.8`.

**Solved example 2.** Eight people average 30 years. Two people aged 40 each join. New average?  
**Fastest method:** `(8×30 + 2×40)/10 = 320/10 = 32 years`.

**Common trap:** Taking the simple mean of group averages when group sizes differ.

**Practice**

- **Q04.1** Find the average of 10, 20, 30, 40.
- **Q04.2** Five scores average 18. Find their total.
- **Q04.3** Four values average 12. Add 20. Find the new average.
- **Q04.4** Five values average 24. Replace a value of 30 with 40. Find the new average.
- **Q04.5** A group of 20 averages 60, another group of 30 averages 70. Find the combined average.

## Q05. Time and Work

**Core concept.** If one person finishes a task in `a` days, the rate is `1/a` task per day. Combine work rates, not completion times. Use unit work or the LCM of given times to avoid fractions.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- Work rate of A `= 1/a`; together A and B `= 1/a + 1/b`.
- Together time `= ab/(a+b)` for two independent, constant positive rates.
- If A and B together take `t` days and A takes `a`, then B's rate `= 1/t - 1/a`.
- For equal worker productivity and fixed task size, `people × days × hours/day` is constant.
- A draining pipe has a **negative** rate.

**Solved example 1.** A takes 12 days and B takes 18. How long together?  
**Fastest method:** `12×18/(12+18) = 216/30 = 7.2 days`.

**Solved example 2.** Together they take 10 days. A alone takes 15 days. B alone?  
**Fastest method:** B's rate `1/10 - 1/15 = 1/30`; answer: **30 days**.

**Common trap:** Using `a+b` for simultaneous work, or forgetting that a leak subtracts from filling rate.

**Practice**

- **Q05.1** A takes 10 days, B takes 15. Find their together time.
- **Q05.2** A completes `1/8` of a job per day. What fraction does A finish in three days?
- **Q05.3** A and B together take 12 days. A alone takes 20. Find B's time.
- **Q05.4** Six identical workers need 15 days. How many days for 10 workers at the same daily hours?
- **Q05.5** A pipe fills a tank in six hours; a drain empties it in nine hours. If both are open, how long to fill an empty tank?

## Q06. Time, Speed, Distance

**Core concept.** Distance is speed multiplied by time. When equal distances are covered at different speeds, the slower part contributes more time. For trains, distance traveled includes train length and sometimes platform length.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- `distance = speed × time`; `time = distance/speed`.
- `km/h → m/s`: multiply by `5/18`; reverse multiply by `18/5`.
- Equal distances at speeds `u` and `v`: average speed `= 2uv/(u+v)`.
- Relative speed: add speeds for opposite directions, subtract for same direction when moving in a line.
- For a train crossing a pole: `time = train length/speed`; crossing a platform: use `train length + platform length`.

**Solved example 1.** A vehicle covers equal distances at 40 km/h and 60 km/h. Average speed?  
**Fastest method:** Harmonic mean `2×40×60/(40+60) = 48 km/h`.

**Solved example 2.** A 150 m train crosses a pole at 54 km/h. Time?  
**Fastest method:** `54×5/18 = 15 m/s`; time `150/15 = 10 seconds`.

**Common trap:** Averaging speeds arithmetically when distances, rather than times, are equal.

**Practice**

- **Q06.1** A car travels 210 km in 3.5 hours. Find speed.
- **Q06.2** Convert 72 km/h to m/s.
- **Q06.3** Equal distances are traveled at 30 km/h and 60 km/h. Find average speed.
- **Q06.4** Two vehicles travel toward each other at 45 km/h and 55 km/h. Find relative speed.
- **Q06.5** A boat's still-water speed is 15 km/h and the current is 3 km/h. Find downstream speed.

## Q07. Simple and Compound Interest

**Core concept.** Simple interest is calculated only on principal. Compound interest adds earned interest to the balance, so later interest can also earn interest. Match the rate to the compounding period.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- Simple interest `SI = PRT/100`; simple interest amount `A = P(1+RT/100)` for annual rate `R%` and time `T` years.
- Annual compounding: `A = P(1+R/100)^T` for whole-year periods.
- Two years, annual compounding: `CI - SI = P(R/100)^2`.
- Half-yearly compounding: halve nominal annual rate and double number of years of periods, **if** the stated rate is nominal annual and compounded twice a year.

**Solved example 1.** ₹10,000 at 10% for two years. Compare SI and annually compounded CI.  
**Fastest method:** SI = `₹2,000`; compound amount `₹10,000×1.1² = ₹12,100`, so CI = **₹2,100**.

**Solved example 2.** Find `CI - SI` on ₹8,000 at 5% for two years with annual compounding.  
**Fastest method:** `8000×(0.05)² = ₹20`.

**Common trap:** Treating nominal 12% compounded half-yearly as 12% per half-year. The periodic rate is 6%.

**Practice**

- **Q07.1** Find SI on ₹6,000 at 8% per year for three years.
- **Q07.2** Find the simple interest amount on ₹5,000 at 10% per year for two years.
- **Q07.3** Find the amount on ₹10,000 at 10% annually compounded for two years.
- **Q07.4** Find `CI - SI` on ₹4,000 at 10% for two years, compounded annually.
- **Q07.5** Find the amount on ₹10,000 at nominal 12% per annum compounded half-yearly for one year.

## Q08. Permutations and Combinations

**Core concept.** A permutation counts arrangements, where order matters. A combination counts selections, where order does not matter. First decide which of these the question asks, then handle restrictions such as repetition or leading zero.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- `n! = n(n-1)...1`, and `0! = 1`.
- Without replacement: `nPr = n!/(n-r)!` for **ordered** choices.
- Without replacement: `nCr = n!/[r!(n-r)!]` for **unordered** choices.
- `nCr = nC(n-r)`; use the smaller of `r` and `n-r`.
- Distinct permutations of a word with repeated letters: `n!/(a!b!...)`.

**Solved example 1.** Five people compete for president and secretary positions. Number of possibilities?  
**Fastest method:** Two distinct roles: `5×4 = 20`.

**Solved example 2.** Choose three people from six for a team, without assigned roles.  
**Fastest method:** `6C3 = 6×5×4/(3×2×1) = 20`.

**Common trap:** Using permutations for committee selections, or treating identical letters as distinguishable.

**Practice**

- **Q08.1** In how many ways can six different people stand in a line?
- **Q08.2** How many two-person teams can be chosen from eight people?
- **Q08.3** How many four-digit codes can be made from digits 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 without repetition? Codes need not be numeric quantities.
- **Q08.4** How many distinct arrangements of the letters in `LEVEL` are possible?
- **Q08.5** How many three-person teams can be chosen from seven people?

## Q09. Probability

**Core concept.** Probability measures how likely an event is on a scale from 0 to 1. For equally likely outcomes, divide favorable outcomes by all outcomes. Use the complement for “at least one” and multiply conditional probabilities for sequential draws.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- For equally likely outcomes: `P(E) = favorable/total`.
- Complement: `P(not E) = 1 - P(E)`.
- Independent events: `P(A and B) = P(A)P(B)`.
- Generally: `P(A and B) = P(A)P(B|A)`.
- For selecting `r` objects without replacement, combinations often simplify counting.

**Solved example 1.** Two fair independent coins are tossed. Probability of exactly one head?  
**Fastest method:** Outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT; favorable 2 of 4. Answer: **1/2**.

**Solved example 2.** A bag has three red and two blue balls. Two are drawn without replacement. Probability both red?  
**Fastest method:** `3C2/5C2 = 3/10`.

**Common trap:** Multiplying unconditional probabilities when draws are dependent, especially without replacement.

**Practice**

- **Q09.1** Roll one fair six-sided die. Probability of an even number?
- **Q09.2** Roll one fair six-sided die. Probability of a number greater than 4?
- **Q09.3** Toss two independent fair coins. Probability of two heads?
- **Q09.4** A bag has four red and three blue balls. Probability a single random draw is blue?
- **Q09.5** Draw two cards from a standard 52-card deck without replacement. Probability both are aces?

## Q10. Number Systems

**Core concept.** Number-system questions frequently test divisibility, factors, multiples, remainders, unit-digit cycles, and trailing zeroes. Recognizing patterns usually beats long arithmetic.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- For positive integers `a` and `b`, `HCF(a,b) × LCM(a,b) = a×b`.
- Remainder arithmetic: `(a+b) mod m` and `(ab) mod m` can use the remainders of the terms.
- Unit digits repeat in cycles, for example powers of 3 end in `3,9,7,1`.
- Trailing zeroes in `n!` = `floor(n/5) + floor(n/25) + floor(n/125) + ...`.
- Divisibility: by 3 or 9, check digit sum; by 11, alternating digit sum difference is divisible by 11.

**Solved example 1.** Find remainder when `7^103` is divided by 6.  
**Fastest method:** `7 ≡ 1 (mod 6)`, so `7^103 ≡ 1`. Answer: **1**.

**Solved example 2.** Number of trailing zeroes in `50!`?  
**Fastest method:** `floor(50/5)+floor(50/25) = 10+2 = 12`.

**Common trap:** Counting only multiples of 5, ignoring extra factors of 5 in 25, 125, and so on.

**Practice**

- **Q10.1** Find the remainder when 35 is divided by 6.
- **Q10.2** Find the units digit of `3^4`.
- **Q10.3** Find the HCF of 24 and 36.
- **Q10.4** Find the LCM of 12 and 18.
- **Q10.5** How many trailing zeroes are in `100!`?

## Q11. Data Interpretation: Tables and Graphs

**Core concept.** Data interpretation tests extraction and arithmetic, not advanced mathematics. Read the title, units, scale, legend, and time period first. Compare quantities with the same unit and base.

**Formulas and shortcuts**

- `% growth = (new-old)/old × 100`; `share% = part/total × 100`.
- `Average = sum/number of entries`.
- For a table, scan row or column totals before doing individual computations.
- For a graph, first check whether the axis starts at zero. A truncated axis can make changes look larger, though calculations must use the actual labels.

**Data table: Quarterly sales in ₹ lakh**

| Quarter | Product A | Product B | Product C |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Q1 | 40 | 50 | 30 |
| Q2 | 60 | 45 | 45 |
| Q3 | 80 | 60 | 60 |
| Q4 | 100 | 75 | 75 |

**Graph: Hires per year, each `#` means 20 hires**

```text
2024 | ##   40
2025 | ###  60
2026 | #### 80
```

**Solved example 1.** Find total sales in Q3.  
**Fastest method:** Read Q3 across: `80+60+60 = ₹200 lakh`.

**Solved example 2.** What is the percentage increase in hires from 2024 to 2026?  
**Fastest method:** From the chart, hires double from 40 to 80, so the increase is **100%**.

**Common trap:** Confusing percentage-point changes with percentage changes, or reading bars without checking their scale.

**Practice**

- **Q11.1** From the table, find combined sales of all three products in Q1.
- **Q11.2** From the table, find percentage growth in Product B sales from Q2 to Q4.
- **Q11.3** From the table, find total annual sales of Product A.
- **Q11.4** From the graph, which year has the highest number of hires, and how many?
- **Q11.5** From the graph, what is the percentage increase in hires from 2024 to 2025?

# Part II. Logical Reasoning

## R01. Number and Letter Series

**Core concept.** Series questions ask you to detect a repeated transformation. Check differences first, then differences of differences, multiplication, division, alternate positions, squares, and alphabet positions.

**Shortcuts**

- Write first differences directly under numbers. For `+2,+4,+6`, the next difference is `+8` if the pattern remains consistent.
- Look for `n²`, `n³`, primes, or alternating subsequences.
- For letters, map `A=1` through `Z=26` and track steps.
- A short finite sequence can admit many rules. Choose the simplest rule consistent with all displayed terms, especially in multiple-choice tests.

**Solved example 1.** `3, 7, 13, 21, 31, ?`  
**Fastest method:** Differences `4,6,8,10`, next `12`. Answer: **43**.

**Solved example 2.** `2, 6, 18, 54, ?`  
**Fastest method:** Multiply by 3 each time. Answer: **162**.

**Common trap:** Fixating on a complicated formula before checking basic differences.

**Practice**

- **R01.1** `4, 9, 16, 25, ?`
- **R01.2** `2, 5, 10, 17, ?`
- **R01.3** `A, C, F, J, O, ?`
- **R01.4** `1, 1, 2, 3, 5, ?`
- **R01.5** `81, 27, 9, 3, ?`

## R02. Coding and Decoding

**Core concept.** A word or number is converted using a consistent rule. Typical rules shift letters, reverse order, replace letters with positions, or apply different rules by position. Derive the mapping from every given example.

**Shortcuts**

- Write `A=1` to `Z=26`; handle wraparound, such as `Z+1=A`, only when the rule specifies it.
- Test simple shifts `+1`, `-1`, `+2`, and reversal before more complex patterns.
- For multiword problems, distinguish letter transformation from word reordering.
- One example does not uniquely establish a code unless the question specifies the rule.

**Solved example 1.** If every letter moves forward two places, code `CAT`.  
**Fastest method:** C→E, A→C, T→V. Answer: **ECV**.

**Solved example 2.** If a word is coded by reversing its letters, code `LAMP`.  
**Fastest method:** Read backward: **PMAL**.

**Common trap:** Applying a guessed pattern to a different question without checking its stated rule.

**Practice**

- **R02.1** Shift each letter forward one place, with Z wrapping to A. Code `SUN`.
- **R02.2** Reverse the letters of `PLAN`.
- **R02.3** Shift each letter forward two places, with wraparound. Code `BAD`.
- **R02.4** If code is the sum of alphabet positions, code `CAB`.
- **R02.5** A code shifts every letter forward one place. Decode `UFTU`.

## R03. Blood Relations

**Core concept.** Convert sentences into a family tree and trace relationships from the named reference person. Separate gender from relationship whenever the question does not specify it.

**Shortcuts**

- Use `↑` for parent, `↓` for child, and a horizontal connector for siblings or spouses.
- Identify the generation first, then whether the relation is maternal or paternal.
- “Brother” specifies the brother's gender, not automatically the gender of other relatives.
- If a gender is unstated, answer “grandparent” or “sibling” rather than guessing.

**Solved example 1.** R is S's mother. S is T's brother. How is R related to T?  
**Fastest method:** S and T are siblings; R is **T's mother**.

**Solved example 2.** P is Q's brother. Q is R's father. How is P related to R?  
**Fastest method:** Father's brother = **paternal uncle**.

**Common trap:** Assuming a person's gender merely from a name or a different person's relationship.

**Practice**

- **R03.1** A is B's father. B is C's mother. How is A related to C?
- **R03.2** M is N's sister. N is P's mother. How is M related to P?
- **R03.3** X is Y's son. Y is Z's daughter. How is X related to Z?
- **R03.4** R is S's mother. S is T's father. How is R related to T?
- **R03.5** A is B's wife. B is C's brother. How is A related to C?

## R04. Directions and Distance

**Core concept.** Convert movement to a coordinate sketch instead of imagining the route. North and south cancel; east and west cancel. Turns are relative to the direction you currently face.

**Shortcuts**

- Coordinates: `E = +x`, `W = -x`, `N = +y`, `S = -y`.
- Shortest straight-line distance `= √(x²+y²)`.
- Right turn rotates clockwise: N→E→S→W. Left turn rotates the other way.
- Final position and final facing direction are separate questions.

**Solved example 1.** Walk 3 km north and 4 km east. How far from start?  
**Fastest method:** 3-4-5 triangle. **5 km northeast**.

**Solved example 2.** Walk 5 m north, turn right for 2 m, then turn right for 5 m. Final position?  
**Fastest method:** North 5 cancels south 5. Remains **2 m east of start**.

**Common trap:** Calculating total path length when the question asks shortest distance.

**Practice**

- **R04.1** Walk 6 m east then 8 m north. Find shortest distance and direction from start.
- **R04.2** Walk 5 m north then 5 m south. Where are you relative to start?
- **R04.3** Facing west, you turn left. Which way are you facing?
- **R04.4** Walk 4 m south then 3 m west. Find shortest distance and direction from start.
- **R04.5** Walk 10 m east, 4 m north, then 10 m west. Final position relative to start?

## R05. Seating Arrangements

**Core concept.** Seating puzzles translate position clues into a diagram. In a straight row, number seats left to right. In circular seating, fix one person as an anchor because rotating everyone does not change the relative arrangement.

**Shortcuts**

- Place definite endpoints or exact gaps before vague “beside” relationships.
- Facing north in a row: person's left matches the reader's left. Facing south: reverse it.
- In a circle, distinguish “facing center” from “facing outward”; left and right reverse.
- Check all clues against the completed layout; use cases if needed.

**Solved example 1.** Five people A, B, C, D, E face north. A is leftmost, B is immediately right of A, C is rightmost, and D is immediately left of C. Arrange them.  
**Fastest method:** Fill fixed positions first: `A B E D C`.

**Solved example 2.** Four people sit around a round table clockwise in order A, B, C, D. Who is opposite A?  
**Fastest method:** Two positions away in a four-seat circle: **C**.

**Common trap:** Treating left and right as absolute rather than relative to how people face.

**Practice**

- **R05.1** Five people face north in the row `A B C D E`. Who is immediately right of C?
- **R05.2** A north-facing row is `Q P R S T`. Who is immediately left of R?
- **R05.3** A north-facing row is `A B C D E F`. How many people sit strictly between B and E?
- **R05.4** Four people sit clockwise as `A B C D` around a circle. Who sits opposite B?
- **R05.5** Five people face north in the row `L M N O P`. Who is second to the left of O?

## R06. Syllogisms

**Core concept.** Syllogisms ask what **must** follow from statements about groups. Do not use outside knowledge. Draw small overlapping circles, or reason using subsets and intersections.

**Shortcuts**

- `All A are B` means A is a subset of B, not that all B are A.
- `Some A are B` means at least one A is B.
- `No A are B` means the groups do not overlap.
- In standard modern logic, an “all” statement alone does not prove that any A exists. Some aptitude providers use different syllogism conventions, so follow instructions on the test.

**Solved example 1.** All analysts are graduates. All graduates are learners. Must all analysts be learners?  
**Fastest method:** Subset chain `Analysts ⊆ Graduates ⊆ Learners`. **Yes**.

**Solved example 2.** Some testers are coders. All coders are engineers. Must some testers be engineers?  
**Fastest method:** The testers who are coders are also engineers. **Yes**.

**Common trap:** Reversing “all,” or treating “some” as “all.”

**Practice**

For each question, decide whether the proposed conclusion **necessarily follows**.

- **R06.1** All cats are mammals. All mammals are animals. Conclusion: All cats are animals.
- **R06.2** Some A are B. All B are C. Conclusion: Some A are C.
- **R06.3** All A are B. Some B are C. Conclusion: Some A are C.
- **R06.4** No A are B. All C are A. Conclusion: No C are B.
- **R06.5** All A are B. Conclusion: Some B are A. Use standard modern logic without an existence assumption.

## R07. Logic Puzzles and Ordering

**Core concept.** Puzzles combine several constraints to find a unique order or assignment. Write a grid or slots, translate each fact into a restriction, and check whether multiple arrangements remain possible.

**Shortcuts**

- Place exact positions first, then adjacency and before/after clues.
- Use elimination and mark impossible cells with `×`.
- Do not assume a unique answer unless the clues force one.
- For rankings, `A > B > C` can be chained immediately.

**Solved example 1.** Amit is taller than Bharat, and Bharat is taller than Chetan. Who is tallest?  
**Fastest method:** Chain `Amit > Bharat > Chetan`. **Amit**.

**Solved example 2.** Tasks A, B, C occur on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday. B is Wednesday and A is before C. Find the schedule.  
**Fastest method:** B fixes Wednesday. A before C fills **Monday A, Tuesday C, Wednesday B**.

**Common trap:** Filling a slot from an assumption rather than a stated constraint, then treating it as certain.

**Practice**

- **R07.1** A ranks above B; B ranks above C. Who ranks second?
- **R07.2** Red, Blue, Green boxes occupy positions 1, 2, 3. Blue is in position 3, and Red is immediately before Blue. Which color is in position 1?
- **R07.3** Maths, English, Science tests occur Mon, Tue, Wed. English is Wed; Maths is before Science. What subject is on Tuesday?
- **R07.4** Dev, Esha, Farid take positions 1, 2, 3. Farid is third, Esha is first. What is Dev's position?
- **R07.5** Floors are 1 to 4 from bottom to top. W lives on 4, X immediately below W, Y on 1, and Z on the remaining floor. Where does Z live?

## R08. Data Sufficiency

**Core concept.** Data sufficiency asks whether the given information is enough to answer a question, not necessarily to compute the answer. Test each statement independently before combining them.

**Answer-code system for this guide**

| Code | Meaning |
|:--|:--|
| A | Statement I alone is sufficient; II alone is not |
| B | Statement II alone is sufficient; I alone is not |
| C | Together sufficient; neither alone sufficient |
| D | Either statement alone is sufficient |
| E | Even together insufficient |

**Shortcuts**

- Ask whether a statement gives a definite **yes or no**, not just a definite yes.
- For a numerical answer, one equation with two unknowns is usually insufficient without another restriction.
- Test counterexamples: if both yes and no remain possible, the information is insufficient.
- Read the test's own codes first. Code letters vary across exams.

**Solved example 1.** Is integer x even? I: x is divisible by 4. II: x is divisible by 2.  
**Fastest method:** Both guarantee evenness alone. Answer **D**.

**Solved example 2.** Is integer n greater than 10? I: n > 8. II: n < 12.  
**Fastest method:** Together n could be 9, 10, or 11; the answer varies. **E**.

**Common trap:** Combining statements before checking whether either one already suffices.

**Practice**

Use the A to E code system above. Variables are integers unless explicitly stated otherwise.

- **R08.1** Is x positive? I: `x > 0`. II: `x² = 9`.
- **R08.2** Is x even? I: x is divisible by 4. II: x is divisible by 6.
- **R08.3** What is x? I: `x+y = 10`. II: `y = 4`.
- **R08.4** Is n even? I: n is positive. II: n is divisible by 10.
- **R08.5** Is x greater than 5? I: `x > 3`. II: `x < 7`.

## R09. Pattern Recognition

**Core concept.** Pattern questions use visual or numerical transformations such as rotation, mirroring, alternating symbols, repeated shapes, and row-to-column rules. Track one property at a time: direction, size, count, shading, or value.

**Shortcuts**

- For a grid, test a rule across the first completed row before applying it to the missing cell.
- Track rotations in 90° steps separately from reflections.
- Separate odd and even positions in alternating patterns.
- A numerical pattern can be inside a visual arrangement. Check simple square, double, or sum relationships first.

**Solved example 1.** A grid maps `2 → 4`, `3 → 9`, and `4 → ?`.  
**Fastest method:** Right value equals left value squared. **16**.

**Solved example 2.** Directions rotate `↑, →, ↓, ←, ?`.  
**Fastest method:** Rotate 90° clockwise each step. **↑**.

**Common trap:** Confusing mirror images with rotations, or ignoring one simultaneous change in a shape.

**Practice**

- **R09.1** Complete the squares pattern `1, 4, 9, 16, ?`.
- **R09.2** Complete `▲, ●, ▲, ●, ?`.
- **R09.3** Complete clockwise directions `N, E, S, W, ?`.
- **R09.4** Apply one rule to `3 → 9`, `5 → 25`, `7 → ?`.
- **R09.5** Find the odd one out by shape category: triangle, square, pentagon, circle.

# Part III. Verbal Ability

## V01. Reading Comprehension

**Core concept.** Reading comprehension tests what a passage states, implies, or is mainly about. Answer from the passage, not general knowledge. Separate the author's claim from what can actually be concluded from the evidence.

**Strategy and shortcuts**

- Read the question first only if it helps focus; for a very short passage, read the whole passage first.
- After each paragraph, mentally label its job: issue, evidence, contrast, or conclusion.
- Main idea = central point that fits the entire passage, not one detail.
- Eliminate options using extreme claims like “always” and “never” unless the text supports them.
- For inference, choose what is justified, not what merely sounds plausible.

**Solved example 1.** Passage: “A firm enabled multifactor authentication. Login took slightly longer, but account-takeover reports declined. The firm retained the control.” What is the main idea?  
**Fastest method:** Combine benefit and cost: **MFA added friction but improved account security, so it was retained**.

**Solved example 2.** Passage: “Thirty volunteers used a new tool for a month and completed tasks faster afterward. There was no comparison group.” Does this prove the tool caused faster performance?  
**Fastest method:** No control group means other causes remain possible. **No, it shows improvement, not proven causation**.

**Common trap:** Choosing a reasonable statement that the passage does not actually support.

**Practice passage**

> An engineering team replaced weekly status meetings with asynchronous written updates. Meeting hours fell, and most members reported more uninterrupted work. However, new joiners found decisions harder to trace because updates were scattered across channels. The team is now testing a single searchable decision log.

- **V01.1** What did the team replace its weekly meetings with?
- **V01.2** Name one reported benefit of the change.
- **V01.3** What difficulty did new joiners report?
- **V01.4** What solution is being tested?
- **V01.5** Does the passage support the claim that every member preferred the new system? Answer yes or no with a short reason.

## V02. Sentence Correction

**Core concept.** Sentence correction tests grammar and clarity. First locate the verb and its true subject. Then check tense, parallel structure, pronouns, modifiers, and prepositions.

**Rules and shortcuts**

- “Each,” “every,” and “the list” take singular verbs when they are the grammatical subject.
- After `did/did not`, use the base verb: `did not know`, not `did not knew`.
- In a list, match grammatical structure: “designing, testing, and deploying.”
- Avoid changing a sentence merely because a less formal alternative sounds more familiar.

**Solved example 1.** “Each of the engineers have a badge.”  
**Fastest method:** Subject is **Each**, singular. Correct: **Each of the engineers has a badge.**

**Solved example 2.** “She enjoys testing, debugging, and to deploy software.”  
**Fastest method:** Keep parallel `-ing` forms. Correct: **She enjoys testing, debugging, and deploying software.**

**Common trap:** Making the verb agree with a nearby plural noun rather than the real subject.

**Practice: Correct each sentence**

- **V02.1** The list of issues are long.
- **V02.2** He did not knew the answer.
- **V02.3** Every employee have an ID.
- **V02.4** She is senior than me.
- **V02.5** The report was submitted by Ravi and I.

## V03. Para Jumbles

**Core concept.** Para jumbles require arranging sentences into a coherent paragraph. Look for the opening sentence, pronoun references, cause-and-effect links, chronological steps, and conclusion markers.

**Rules and shortcuts**

- Opening sentences tend to introduce a subject without referring backward using “this” or “therefore.”
- Link pairs: problem→effect, action→outcome, noun→pronoun, and earlier→later.
- “Finally,” “as a result,” and “therefore” usually require a preceding idea.
- Build a few strong pairs before trying every possible full order.

**Solved example 1.** A: Finally, the report was published. B: First, the team collected survey responses. C: Next, it cleaned the data. D: Then, it analyzed the results.  
**Fastest method:** Follow explicit chronology. **B-C-D-A**.

**Solved example 2.** A: This can create duplicate records. B: A user refreshes a payment page. C: An idempotency key prevents repeat processing. D: The request may be sent twice.  
**Fastest method:** Cause→mechanism→consequence→prevention. **B-D-A-C**.

**Common trap:** Selecting an opening sentence that begins with “This,” “They,” or “Therefore” before establishing its reference.

**Practice: Arrange each set of sentences**

- **V03.1** A: Finally, she submitted the form. B: She opened the portal. C: Then she filled in the details. D: Next she uploaded her resume.
- **V03.2** A: Therefore, battery life improved. B: The app repeatedly polled the server. C: The team reduced polling frequency. D: Frequent requests drained power.
- **V03.3** A: A customer reported a crash. B: Developers identified a null reference. C: They reproduced the crash. D: After testing, the fix was released.
- **V03.4** A: This made the result easier to verify. B: The analyst recorded each assumption. C: Next, she documented the formula. D: Finally, she attached the raw data.
- **V03.5** A: A second billing counter opened. B: The canteen was crowded at lunch. C: As a result, the queue became shorter. D: Orders were processed in parallel.

## V04. Fill in the Blanks

**Core concept.** Choose words based on grammar, meaning, and tone. Read the entire sentence before selecting an option. Contrast words such as “although” and “despite” can reverse the expected direction.

**Rules and shortcuts**

- Predict the missing word in your own words before viewing choices.
- Check whether the blank needs a noun, verb, adjective, or adverb.
- Use nearby clues: “however” marks contrast; “therefore” marks result.
- Eliminate grammatically invalid words before comparing fine shades of meaning.

**Solved example 1.** “Despite the delay, the team ____ with the release.”  
**Fastest method:** “Despite” signals persistence. **proceeded**.

**Solved example 2.** “The explanation was so ____ that beginners understood it.”  
**Fastest method:** Result “beginners understood” implies **clear**.

**Common trap:** Picking a word that is positive or familiar but does not fit the sentence's logical connection.

**Practice: Choose the best option**

- **V04.1** The auditor requested ____ evidence before closing the finding. (A) relevant (B) irrelevant (C) imaginary (D) unrelated
- **V04.2** The deadline is tomorrow; the team must act ____. (A) rarely (B) promptly (C) lazily (D) randomly
- **V04.3** Although the data was limited, she avoided making ____ claims. (A) modest (B) careful (C) unsupported (D) qualified
- **V04.4** The network outage was ____; service returned in two minutes. (A) permanent (B) endless (C) chronic (D) brief
- **V04.5** The team chose a ____ system to support increasing traffic. (A) fragile (B) scalable (C) obsolete (D) rigid

## V05. Vocabulary in Context

**Core concept.** Word meaning depends on its sentence. Identify whether the word signals strength, weakness, certainty, timing, or causality. Prefer the option matching the local meaning rather than the most common dictionary meaning.

**Rules and shortcuts**

- Replace the target word with a simple synonym and reread the sentence.
- Recognize useful workplace terms: `mitigate = reduce impact`, `ambiguous = unclear`, `concise = brief`, `robust = resilient/strong`.
- Prefixes help, but are not always decisive: `in-` may mean “not,” `inter-` means “between.”
- Watch context-sensitive words: “redundant” can mean unnecessary repetition or intentional backup.

**Solved example 1.** “The patch mitigated the risk.” What does “mitigated” mean?  
**Fastest method:** It made the risk less severe. **Reduced**, not necessarily eliminated.

**Solved example 2.** “The instruction was ambiguous.” Meaning?  
**Fastest method:** It allowed more than one interpretation. **Unclear**.

**Common trap:** Choosing a synonym that is too strong, such as “eliminated” instead of “reduced.”

**Practice: Give a short meaning of the bold word**

- **V05.1** “The evidence was **inconclusive**.”
- **V05.2** “The training is **mandatory**.”
- **V05.3** “The fault was **intermittent**.”
- **V05.4** “She gave a **concise** summary.”
- **V05.5** “A second identical approval step added no protection and was **redundant**.”

# Timed-Test Strategy: 60 to 90 Minutes

**First confirm the test rules:** number of questions, section locks, navigation rules, negative marking, calculator availability, and whether skipped questions can be revisited. These change what “optimal” means.

## Initial time budget

| Question style | First-pass target | Skip or flag when |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Direct arithmetic or formula | 30 to 60 seconds | No viable setup after ~30 seconds |
| Multi-step quantitative | 60 to 90 seconds | Repeated arithmetic or no clear method after ~75 seconds |
| Simple reasoning | 30 to 60 seconds | Rule or diagram still unclear after ~45 seconds |
| Seating or multi-clue puzzle | 75 to 120 seconds | Multiple cases remain and expected payoff is low |
| Sentence, vocabulary, fill blank | 20 to 45 seconds | Two options remain with no distinguishing clue |
| Reading comprehension | Read short passage once, then 30 to 60 seconds per question | Need repeated full rereads for one question |

A **global average budget** is `total test minutes × 60 / question count`. For example, 60 questions in 75 minutes gives 75 seconds per question on average. Longer reading passages or grouped DI sets may justify more time. If sections have separate timers, allocate within each section rather than borrowing time from another.

## Three-pass approach, only when navigation permits

1. **Pass 1, easy marks:** Solve direct questions immediately. Flag any that lack a clear method after about half of their usual time budget.
2. **Pass 2, workable questions:** Return to medium problems, DI sets, and puzzles with structured clues. Avoid sinking several minutes into a single low-confidence item.
3. **Pass 3, review:** Check units, bases, negative signs, and unmarked questions. Submit remaining guesses only if the scoring rules support guessing.

## Guessing without wasting marks

- **No negative marking:** If allowed, answer every question before time expires. Eliminate options when possible.
- **Negative marking:** Guess when your estimated probability of being correct is greater than `penalty / (reward + penalty)`, where reward is marks gained for correct and penalty is the positive magnitude of marks lost for incorrect. Example: `+1/-0.25` gives threshold `0.25/1.25 = 20%`; this assumes zero for blank answers.
- **Four equally likely choices:** A blind guess has 25% success probability. If you eliminate two genuinely wrong options, a choice between the remaining two has 50% chance, assuming no better information.
- **Do not assume:** All EY or Deloitte tests have the same marking, navigation, or calculator rules.

## Speed priorities

Memorize common fraction-percentage conversions, squares from 1 to 30, multiplication tables, basic divisibility rules, and speed conversion. Practice setting up equations, estimating answers to reject options, and checking whether the requested value is a rate, total, ratio, or percentage.

# Ten Common Mistakes Under Time Pressure

1. **Wrong percentage base:** Using the new value when the old value is the denominator.
2. **Adding successive percentages:** Treating 20% up and 20% down as no change.
3. **Mixing units:** Using km/h with metres, or hours with seconds, without converting.
4. **Averaging averages blindly:** Ignoring different group sizes or travel times.
5. **Using the wrong counting rule:** Permutations for unordered groups, or ignoring repeated letters.
6. **Assuming independence:** Treating without-replacement draws like independent draws.
7. **Making hidden assumptions:** Inventing genders, positions, existence of groups, or puzzle rules.
8. **Reading too much into passages:** Using outside knowledge rather than textual support.
9. **Getting stuck on one hard question:** Losing several easier questions to one puzzle.
10. **Ignoring test instructions:** Missing negative marking, section locks, navigation limits, or tool restrictions.
